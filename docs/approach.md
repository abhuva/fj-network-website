# Start with the person.<br><em>Explore the possibilities.</em>

<p class="page-lead">We explore how circus and movement activities can respond to different bodies, interests, ways of communicating and ways of learning.</p>

## What that looks like

Sometimes the useful change is small: a different ball, a slower pace, a shared surface, a choice of position. Sometimes it means rethinking the activity together.

Rather than setting one route through a task, we can offer different starting points. Rolling, balancing, passing and catching can each become an invitation to play, make choices and connect with someone else.

## Our connection to Functional Juggling

Functional Juggling is a central influence on this network. Craig Quat’s work explores access to juggling through adaptive equipment and different ways of facilitating participation. The juggleboard is one familiar example. [Learn about Craig’s work at Quat Props](https://www.quatprops.net/).

Our network also brings together people working with other circus and movement practices. We want space for these approaches to meet, while keeping their origins and contributors visible.

<aside class="note" markdown="1">
**One example: a shared board**

A facilitator can change the distance, speed or arrangement of rolling balls, or invite participants to choose a pattern together. The board offers a shared surface; the activity develops through the people using it. This is an illustration of an approach, rather than a session plan or a promise of a particular outcome.
</aside>

## Principles we propose to work by

1. **Participation includes choice.** People can influence the activity, change how they take part, pause or stop.
2. **Adaptation belongs in the design.** We consider the materials, space, pace and communication from the beginning.
3. **Experience deserves reflection.** We document the context, what we tried, what happened and what remains uncertain.
4. **Different knowledge belongs here.** Participants, facilitators and partner organisations each bring useful perspectives.
5. **Sharing includes credit.** We name contributors and explain the conditions for reusing their work.

These principles are draft proposals for discussion across the network.

## Learning from practice

Case studies offer descriptions of particular experiences. They help us ask better questions about our own settings; they do not establish that an activity will have the same effect elsewhere.
